Engineering representations that make models work

In a time series anomaly detection project on real network traffic data, I worked on building an LSTM-based model to identify abnormal patterns in IDC port activity.

The data was messy, non-stationary, and full of irregular spikes — the kind of signals that don’t behave nicely in textbook examples.


What stood out was not just model performance, but how much of the work was actually about reshaping the data: constructing meaningful temporal windows, extending feature dimensions, and making the signal “learnable” in the first place.

Once the features were properly engineered, the model was finally able to make sense of the temporal structure in the data. This directly improved reconstruction quality and led to a high-recall anomaly detection system — which was critical given how sparse true anomaly labels were in real-world traffic.

Birds know something we don’t

In a multi-source climate and migration project, we studied barn swallow migration patterns using environmental signals and time series modeling.

One observation stood out: migration timing was more strongly aligned with the future temperature of their destination — and even the future climate of their origin — rather than the local conditions at their current location.

In other words, their behavior seemed more connected to where the climate was going than where they were.

This changed how I think about data. Sometimes the most interesting patterns are not inside the model — but in what the model forces you to notice about the world.
